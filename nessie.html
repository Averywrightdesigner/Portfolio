Introduction

The mental health app space has mainly focused on crisis intervention, clinical therapy models, and broad audiences, often prioritizing diagnosis, tracking, or professional treatment over everyday emotional support for students. What existing products and services fail to address is the need for private, low-pressure support that students can access outside of traditional counseling, especially for stress, isolation, and day-to-day emotional challenges.

Nessie addresses this gap as a privacy-first mental health app that supports students through gentle guidance, emotional check-ins, and secure interactions, without pressure or judgment. Our focus is college students who deal with stress, feelings of isolation, or hesitation toward traditional mental health resources, especially those looking for support that fits into their daily routines. If Nessie were on the market, success would look like repeat use of its support features, more comfort expressing feelings privately, and feedback showing reduced stress or improved well-being.

My role

I pitched the idea for Nessie to a class of 90 students, built a team of six capable designers, and led the project as Project Manager. To keep the 3-month project running smoothly, I set weekly goals in Teams and checked in on each teammate's progress.

Before we submitted any work, we reviewed each other's writing, made sure we all agreed, and then made final edits for grammar and a consistent style across our documents. We also wrote a team equity charter that stated our measurable goals, each member's strengths, and rules for handling conflict.

  • 2xa week in class on Mondays and Wednesdays, working together on documents and prototypes
  • 1 hrmeeting I scheduled outside of class each week to talk through progress and concerns, since we all work and go to school full time
  • Teamsgroup chat to keep communication going between meetings

Process

  1. Setting up the team

    I started by creating our team in Microsoft Teams, where we shared files, chatted, and planned virtual meetings. I posted announcements and reminders there, and we used its calendar to track due dates and progress. I also led our FigJam file, where we began with a problem statement to keep us grounded in what we were trying to solve.

  2. Competitive analysis

    We compared 7 products in the mental health space, including BetterHelp, Headspace, and Finch, and listed the pros and cons of each.

  3. User interviews

    Next, we interviewed 7 potential users, college students and recent grads, to understand their behaviors, attitudes, aptitudes, motivations, environments, tools, and challenges. I interviewed one of them, and her answers shaped several of Nessie's features.

    What she told meWhat I designed

    • Deep breaths got her through her last overwhelming moment at work.

      →

      A guided breathing exercise, plus a screen explaining how it works and why it helps

    • Habits like journaling fall off when she's tired.

      →

      Streaks and rewards that take seconds a day

    • She wants an app to feel safe and secure.

      →

      Login with Face ID or a 4-digit PIN

  4. Affinity map

    We grouped what we learned into habits and behaviors, pain points, demographics, needs and wants, and tools.

  5. Domain research

    I then researched the product domain, including current opinions in blogs and articles, user reviews of similar products, and anything else that could shape it. For our literature review, I tracked down and assessed an academic paper on failures in the design and implementation of mental health apps, and wrote up how it applied to Nessie. To improve Nessie, we first needed to know what to avoid.

    The paper recommends three things: co-design with end users from the start, measure practical outcomes like engagement, and design inclusively for underserved populations.

  6. Persona

    I identified user roles, workflows, and behavioral variables, then mapped patterns in our users' feelings, attitudes, goals, skills, frustrations, and environments. With those patterns in mind, we created our persona, Faye Pearl.

    What Faye needs from Nessie

    • Simple reminders and task lists that work around a busy schedule, so an assignment doesn't slip by during a work shift
    • Tasks broken into small steps, with progress feedback that doesn't add pressure
    • Quick grounding exercises and short journaling prompts for overwhelming moments
    • An anonymous space to vent and connect with peers, so she feels less alone

  7. Vision statement

    With our research complete, I looked back at the problem statement and wrote a vision statement to guide our prototyping:

    "The new design of Nessie will help users improve their mental well-being by allowing them to seek support with greater emphasis on daily mental health challenges and privacy needs, and without the pressure or judgment that they currently experience. This will help close the gap in mental health support for students and lead to repeated use of Nessie."

  8. Prototyping

    I gathered the team to start prototyping with simple doodles, mood boards, and brief notes. We discussed what supported our vision statement and what we could leave out, which set expectations for how the app could look. In Figma, we built a style sheet with color scales from 100 to 900 and font styles, and used variables to keep our designs consistent.

    Our wireframes outlined the app's layout, what information each screen would show, and which parts would be interactive. Then we designed the final version, and I created the illustrations of Nessie. We tracked our progress in Google Docs and Teams the whole way, and presented the final prototype at KSU's Capstone showcase.

Testing

We tested with 3 participants ages 18 to 22. Each one thought out loud through 6 tasks.

What we found

  • Every participant tried to use features we hadn't prototyped yet, like typing a journal entry.
  • Participants loved the streaks and points and wanted even more from them.

What we changed

  • We added working interactions to the sign in, journal, community, and profile pages.
  • Streaks and achievements were redesigned to feel more like a game. I prototyped those screens and animations.

Result

Going in, my goals were to create a private, secure space that's free and easy to use, where students can check in on their mental health, and to collaborate as a team on research and prototyping while building a strong portfolio piece and growing as a leader. I believe I checked those boxes.

The hard work paid off. Not only did my design skills improve, but so did my leadership and collaboration. I learned a lot about my communication style, and how staying organized and communicating well makes the design process run smoother. Working through challenges and conflicts is part of the journey, and it helps designs become even more successful.

If I went back now with everything I've learned, it would be even better! I'm so grateful I got to lead this design journey with my teammates and see my idea come to life. Nessie turned out even better than I imagined.
